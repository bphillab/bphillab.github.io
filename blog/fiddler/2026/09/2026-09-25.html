This Week's Fiddler: Sep 25, 2026

Posted on Sep 25, 2026

Problem

Congratulations—you just won your school's tabletop football championship! You flicked your lucky paper football to victory countless times, and would now like to frame it for posterity.

The football is an equilateral triangle with a side length of \(1\) inch. What is the side length of the smallest square frame that will contain the football?

Main Solution

Framing an object efficiently is an exercise in minimizing enclosing dimensions and wasted material. We want to find the smallest square frame (with side length \(s\)) that completely bounds an equilateral triangle of side length \(1\).

A natural starting inclination is to align one edge of the triangle flush against the bottom edge of the frame. In this standard horizontal configuration:

  • The horizontal width is the base edge: \(W = 1\).
  • The vertical height is the triangle's altitude: \(H = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.8660\).

Because the frame must be square, its side length is constrained by the larger of the two perpendicular spans: \(\max(W, H) = \max(1, \sqrt{3}/2) = 1\). However, keeping one edge horizontal leaves considerable dead space along the height (\(1 - \sqrt{3}/2 \approx 0.134\) inches of unused vertical gap). We can achieve greater spatial efficiency by tilting the triangle.

Let the orientation angle \(\theta\) represent the angle of one triangle edge relative to the horizontal axis. Place the triangle vertices at:

\[ \begin{aligned} V_0 &= (0, 0), \\ V_1 &= (\cos\theta, \sin\theta), \\ V_2 &= \left(\cos\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{3}\right), \sin\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{3}\right)\right). \end{aligned} \]

For any angle \(\theta \in [0, \pi/6]\) (that is, \(0^\circ \le \theta \le 30^\circ\)), all three vertices lie in the first quadrant. The horizontal span (width) and vertical span (height) of the triangle are:

\[ \begin{aligned} W(\theta) &= \max(0, \cos\theta, \cos(\theta + \pi/3)) - \min(0, \cos\theta, \cos(\theta + \pi/3)) = \cos\theta, \\ H(\theta) &= \max(0, \sin\theta, \sin(\theta + \pi/3)) - \min(0, \sin\theta, \sin(\theta + \pi/3)) = \sin\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \cos\left(\theta - \frac{\pi}{6}\right). \end{aligned} \]

The smallest square frame enclosing the triangle at orientation \(\theta\) must have side length

\[ S(\theta) = \max(W(\theta), H(\theta)) = \max\left(\cos\theta, \cos\left(\theta - \frac{\pi}{6}\right)\right). \]

As \(\theta\) increases from \(0^\circ\) to \(30^\circ\):

  • \(W(\theta) = \cos\theta\) decreases strictly from \(1\) to \(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\).
  • \(H(\theta) = \cos(\theta - \pi/6)\) increases strictly from \(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) to \(1\).

By symmetry and monotonicity, the minimum of the upper envelope occurs precisely where the width and height are equal:

\[ \cos\theta = \cos\left(\theta - \frac{\pi}{6}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = \frac{\pi}{12} = 15^\circ. \]

At this optimal tilt of \(15^\circ\) (or equivalently \(45^\circ\) or \(75^\circ\) under rotational symmetry), all four interior walls of the square frame make contact with the triangle: two vertices touch the bottom and left edges, while the remaining two vertices touch the top and right edges. The required side length is minimized:

\[ S_{\min} = \cos\left(15^\circ\right) = \frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{2 + \sqrt{3}}}{2} \approx 0.9659258\dots \text{ inches.} \]

Answer: \( \boxed{\frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4}} \approx \boxed{0.9659} \) inches.

Tilting the football reduces the required frame area from \(1.000\text{ in}^2\) down to \(\cos^2(15^\circ) = \frac{2+\sqrt{3}}{4} \approx 0.9330\text{ in}^2\), an area savings of approximately \(6.7\%\).

This Week's Extra Credit

To add a little excitement to the framing process, your new plan is to spin the triangular football by a random angle, and then frame it with a square that is not rotated. That is, the square consists of two perfectly horizontal sides and two perfectly vertical sides.

On average, what can you expect the side length of the smallest resulting square frame to be?

Extra Credit Solution

When the orientation angle \(\Theta\) is drawn uniformly at random from \([0, 2\pi)\), we want the expected side length \(\mathbb{E}[S(\Theta)]\).

Because an equilateral triangle has 3-fold rotational symmetry and reflection symmetry, and an axis-aligned square has 4-fold rotational symmetry and reflection symmetry, the side length function \(S(\theta)\) repeats every \(\pi/6\) radians (\(30^\circ\)) and is mirror-symmetric about \(\pi/12\) (\(15^\circ\)). Thus, the global expectation across all \(360^\circ\) reduces to the average value of \(\cos\theta\) on the fundamental domain \([0, \pi/12]\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[S] &= \frac{1}{\pi/12} \int_0^{\pi/12} \cos\theta \, d\theta \\ &= \frac{12}{\pi} \Big[ \sin\theta \Big]_0^{\pi/12} \\ &= \frac{12}{\pi} \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{12}\right). \end{aligned} \]

Using the exact half-angle formula \(\sin(15^\circ) = \frac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{4}\), we obtain:

\[ \mathbb{E}[S] = \frac{12}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{4} = \frac{3(\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2})}{\pi} \approx 0.9886159\dots \text{ inches.} \]

Extra credit answer: \( \boxed{\frac{3(\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2})}{\pi}} \approx \boxed{0.9886} \) inches.

Distribution of the Frame Size

Beyond the expected value, the entire probability distribution of the frame side length can be characterized analytically. For any side length \(s\) in the interval \([\cos(15^\circ), 1]\):

\[ F(s) = \mathbb{P}(S \le s) = \mathbb{P}(\cos\theta \le s) = 1 - \frac{12}{\pi} \arccos(s). \]

Differentiating with respect to \(s\) gives the probability density function (PDF):

\[ f(s) = \frac{dF}{ds} = \frac{12}{\pi \sqrt{1 - s^2}}, \qquad \frac{\sqrt{6}+\sqrt{2}}{4} \le s \le 1. \]

Because the derivative of the cosine function vanishes at \(\theta = 0\), the density \(f(s)\) diverges as \(s \to 1^-\). As a result, random spins cluster disproportionately near the maximum frame size of \(1\) inch, pulling the median (\(\approx 0.9914\) in) and mean (\(\approx 0.9886\) in) close to the upper limit.
